# First optimality proof: two squares

Status: **formalized and successfully compiled in Lean on 2026-10-30**. See [TwoSquares.lean](../lean/TwoSquares.lean) and the [environment and axiom record](../lean/README.md).

The learning target is the classical result $s(2)=2$, including independently rotated unit squares and boundary contact. This value is recorded in [Friedman's survey](https://erich-friedman.github.io/papers/squares/squares.html). The argument below is written out as a small self-contained formalization target; no novelty is claimed.

## Statements to prove

Use $\operatorname{Packs}(n,L)$ from [the problem definition](01-problem.md). Prove:

1. $\operatorname{Packs}(2,2)$.
2. For every real $L$, $\operatorname{Packs}(2,L)$ implies $2\leq L$.

These establish an attained best side of two without first introducing an infimum. The key lower-bound lemma is:

> Every closed unit square contained in $[0,L]^2$, for $L<2$, contains the container's center in its interior.

The strict word **interior** matters because two squares are allowed to share boundary points.

## Coordinates and interior membership

Take a square centered at $p=(x,y)$ with orthonormal edge directions

$$
u=(c,s),\qquad v=(-s,c),\qquad c^2+s^2=1.
$$

Its closed set is

$$
Q=\{p+\alpha u+\beta v:|\alpha|\leq1/2,\ |\beta|\leq1/2\}.
$$

Because the edge basis is orthonormal, a point $z$ has local coordinates

$$
\alpha=(z-p)\cdot u,\qquad\beta=(z-p)\cdot v.
$$

Both strict inequalities $\lvert\alpha\rvert<1/2$ and $\lvert\beta\rvert<1/2$ imply $z\in\operatorname{int}(Q)$. The Lean proof establishes that the strict-coordinate set is open by continuity of the two projections, then uses its inclusion in the closed square. It is a geometric prerequisite rather than an arithmetic shortcut.

## Containment bounds

Set

$$
q=|c|+|s|,\qquad h=q/2.
$$

The minimum and maximum horizontal coordinates of $Q$ are $x-h$ and $x+h$; vertically they are $y-h$ and $y+h$. Thus containment gives

$$
h\leq x\leq L-h,\qquad h\leq y\leq L-h.
$$

Since $c^2+s^2=1$, we have $q>0$. Define the container center $m=(L/2,L/2)$ and $D=L/2-h$. The bounds imply $D\geq 0$ and

$$
|m_x-x|\leq D,\qquad |m_y-y|\leq D.
$$

No restriction on the signs of $c$ and $s$ has been introduced, so the argument covers every orientation.

## The unavoidable-center lemma

The triangle inequality gives

$$
|(m-p)\cdot u|\leq (|c|+|s|)D=qD.
$$

The same bound holds for the projection onto $v$, because its coefficients have the same sum of absolute values. Now use $L<2$ and $q>0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
qD
&=\frac{Lq-q^2}{2}\\
&<\frac{2q-q^2}{2}\\
&=\frac12-\frac{(q-1)^2}{2}\\
&\leq\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both local coordinates of $m$ therefore have absolute value strictly below $1/2$. Hence $m$ lies in the interior of $Q$.

The proof uses the normalization $c^2+s^2=1$ to obtain a genuine unit square, an orthonormal coordinate system, and $q>0$. The final scalar inequality itself only needs $q>0$ and $L<2$.

## Excluding every smaller packing

Suppose two unit squares fit in a container with $L<2$. Apply the lemma to each square. Their interiors both contain the same point $m$, contradicting pairwise interior disjointness. Therefore every feasible side satisfies $L\geq 2$.

This is a global argument: it considers all centers and orientations, including layouts never generated by a simulator. It does not rely on sampling or a fixed contact pattern.

## Construction at side two

Choose two axis-aligned squares with centers $(1/2,1/2)$ and $(3/2,1/2)$. Their closed sets are $[0,1]\times[0,1]$ and $[1,2]\times[0,1]$. Both lie inside $[0,2]^2$. They share the edge at $x=1$, while their interiors are disjoint. Hence $\operatorname{Packs}(2,2)$.

Together with the lower bound, this proves the best side is exactly two. It does not assert uniqueness of the optimal layout.
